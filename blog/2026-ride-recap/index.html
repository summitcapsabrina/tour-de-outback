2026 Tour de Outback: Ride Recap

A few hundred riders make the drive out to Lakeview every year, and every year they come back with the same look on their faces — the one that says nobody back home is going to believe this. The 2026 Oregon Tour de Outback delivered a full weekend of it. Riders checked in at the Lake County Fairgrounds Friday evening, rolled out just after 7:00 the next morning across five routes into the high desert, and by the time the last bike came back through the gate, the day had produced a storm front you could watch build from ten miles out, a finish-line bouquet picked from the roadside that morning, and a live band playing under the tent well past sundown.

If you rode it, this is your souvenir. If you didn't, here's an honest account of what you missed, and why the 2027 date, June 26, is worth putting on the calendar now instead of waiting to see next year's photos.

Friday Night at the Fairgrounds

The event starts a full day before anyone clips in. Friday afternoon is packet pickup and a warm-up ride to shake out travel legs, followed by a beer garden that opens mid-afternoon and doesn't let up until the pizza comes out. This year the pizza party ran long, with a local band setting up under the event tent and playing well after the sun dropped behind the hills while riders swapped stories about routes they hadn't ridden yet.

Out in the RV loop, someone always ends up cooking dinner over an open flame instead of waiting in line for the official spread, which is its own kind of tradition by now. By the time the raffle and announcements wrapped up, most of the fairgrounds had gone quiet, save for a few stragglers determined to get one more lap of the beer garden in before an early wake-up call.

Saturday, Rolling Out

Ride day starts early and stays organized: a rider briefing covering routes, aid stations, and anything the weather might be doing, followed by a rollout where all five routes leave together before splitting off toward their own distances. Watching a few hundred riders funnel out of the fairgrounds and onto Lakeview's main street that morning is one of those moments photos never quite capture, though people keep trying. Within the first few miles the group thins out along the tree-lined roads west of town, road bikes and gravel rigs finding their own pace before the routes properly diverge.

Five Routes, One Wild Landscape

Tour de Outback earns its name honestly. The gravel routes, a 36-mile intro and a 48-mile step up, climb through ranch roads and public land that most Oregonians never see, let alone ride a bike through. The road routes range from a 40-mile easy spin to a full 105-mile century, and every distance in between rewards riders with the same thing: empty roads, big sky, and terrain that changes character every ten miles. Every route is stocked with aid stations and SAG support, which matters more than it sounds like it should once you're thirty miles into sagebrush country with the nearest town an hour behind you.

Riders who've done other gravel events in the Pacific Northwest tend to say the same thing about this one: there's no traffic to fight, no subdivisions to pedal through to get to the good part, and nothing between you and the horizon once the road turns to gravel.

The Storm That Made the Ride

Weather in Lake County does not do subtle, and 2026 proved it. Riders on the back half of the course watched a wall of dark cloud build over the mountains and roll toward the road they were on, visibly, the way you'd watch a wave come in from the water. Nobody got hurt and nobody got stuck, but everyone who rode into that front came back with a story, and more than a few came back soaked and grinning about it. It's the kind of moment that turns a long ride into a memorable one. One photo of that exact storm turned out good enough to win this year's Photo Contest — more on that below.

Land That Steals the Show

Between the storm and the sunshine, Lake County kept doing what it does best: making riders stop pedaling just to look at something. Sunset over a meadow near the mountains turned the sky the color of a campfire, and roadside stretches of wildflowers that had barely opened by ride weekend were in full bloom by the time the last riders came through.

Riders who paused on the overlooks along the gravel routes got the best of it — a few extra minutes off the bike, mountains in every direction, and nothing but wind and birdsong to fill the silence. This is the part of the event that's genuinely hard to describe to someone who hasn't been out here: the emptiness, the color, the total absence of anything competing for your attention. It's why people who've ridden Tour de Outback once tend to come back.

By the time riders started rolling back down out of the high country, the light had gone long and gold, and the fairgrounds were already filling up with the first finishers.

The Finish Line, and What Came After

Riders started rolling back through the fairgrounds gate around midday, and the finish line turned into exactly the scene you'd hope for: hugs, cold drinks, and a bouquet of wildflowers someone picked off the side of the road and handed to a friend right at the line. Post-ride lunch kept the party going, and by early afternoon the beer garden had refilled with riders comparing notes on which route they'd tackle next year.

Families showed up too, some on bikes and some just along for the ride, including at least one very patient golden retriever who spent the afternoon posing next to a very red bike while the rest of the family took turns holding the newest member of the crew.

By late afternoon, tired legs and sunburned faces were still finding the energy for one more photo in the meadow behind the fairgrounds before everyone packed up for the drive home. That's the thing about this event that's hard to put in a registration form: nobody's in a hurry to leave.

Photo Contest Winners

Every year riders submit their best shots from the road, and every year it gets harder to pick winners. This year the field came down to two photos, and both earned the win outright.
